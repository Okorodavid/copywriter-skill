# Lessons from Harry Dry, Tyson 4D and Stefan Georgi

Copy rules drawn from five YouTube videos (sources at the end). Facts in [brackets] are placeholders. Fill them only with true details from the user, or cut the line.

## Harry Dry: clear, concrete, provable

### The three tests

Run every line through three questions:
1. **Can I visualise it?** Concrete words stick. Abstract words slide off.
2. **Can I falsify it?** A claim that could be proved true or false earns attention. Vague claims get skipped.
3. **Can nobody else say it?** If a rival could sign the same line, rewrite it. Dry credits this test to the ad man Jim Durfee.

- Three no's means filler. Weak: "We make bookkeeping easy."
- Stronger: "Snap a photo of the receipt. It's in your books by [9am] tomorrow."

### Zoom in: abstract to concrete

- Write the vague phrase at the top. Keep asking "what do I actually mean?" Stop at something you could hold or watch happen.
- His example: "get fit again" narrowed to going from the couch to a 5K run, which named the app "Couch to 5K".
- Generic: "better dental health" becomes "no new fillings at your next check-up".

### You can only point

Harry Dry: "You can't talk, you can only point."

- Describe only what a reader could see. Not "she's dedicated" but "she answers client emails on Sunday".
- Swap each adjective for the fact that earned it, or cut it.

### The two-second test

- Show the line and count two seconds. If they don't get it by then, it is too slow.
- Clarity first. Clever lines only land when the reader already knows the product.

### A-to-B process

- Name what the reader believes now (A) and what you want them to believe (B).
- **Who am I talking to?** Picture the moment they meet the words: on a train, passing a shop sign.
- **Do I have something to say?** A real belief or reason.
- **Say it well.** Rhythm, contrast, short words.

### Write it where it lives

- Draft an email in the email tool, an ad in the design tool, a billboard beside other billboards. Surroundings change how words read.
- Start from one seed fact, borrow the layout of an ad you admire, then rewrite. His course ad took about 25 rewrites.
- For feedback, show 2 or 3 versions side by side.

### Conflict and enemies

- People judge by comparison. Draw a line down a page and list opposites.
- Enemies: a different approach, a different belief, a competitor. Before/after and problem/fix count too.
- In cold business messages, make "the usual way" the enemy. Naming rivals can read as petty.

### More rules from Dry

- Stretch the time frame to make a small number big (hours a day become thousands over a career), so the ask looks small.
- Explain the new by comparing it to the known.
- The first line has one job: get the second line read.
- Start from a fact, then build the story on it.
- Every word that isn't helping is hurting (Kaplan's law of words).
- Wider scope, weaker idea. An "and" in a headline is a warning. Do one thing.
- If a sentence can come out and the paragraph still works, cut it.
- Keep paragraphs to two lines or less. Simple writing comes from rewriting.
- Split a big topic into 2 or 3 named, matching parts.
- Open a newsletter with the time, the place and what is happening.
- It is done when no word can be added or removed.
- He sees AI as a research tool, since taste comes from lived experience. Build copy on facts the user supplies. Never invent experiences.

## Tyson 4D: copy basics and cold outreach

### Copy basics

- Three stages: disrupt, intrigue, click. Answer the reader's silent questions: what is this, why keep reading, what next?
- Five openers: challenge a common belief, ask an either-or question, describe their situation, ask a paradox question, make a specific promise.
- Open a loop: hint at the answer, delay it with proof, close it at the call to action.
- Emotion buys, logic justifies. Give one reason of each kind.
- Avoiding a loss moves people more than chasing a gain.
- Show what's in it for the reader within three lines. Write so a 10-year-old could follow.
- Value rises with the dream outcome and the odds of getting it, and falls with the wait and the effort (he credits Alex Hormozi).
- Write to one specific person, in the brand's voice.
- Email shapes: AIDA, PAS (problem, agitate, solution), challenge-justify-need, hook-story-offer.
- Keep subject lines under about 40 characters. A call to action can be "just reply".

### Cold outreach: avoid

- A salesy subject line, or generic praise.
- Talking about yourself in line two.
- In message one: your job title (it invites "we don't need one"), your price, or a call request.
- Insulting their current marketing.
- Padding.

### Cold outreach: do

Four parts:
1. A subject like a friend would write: "quick question, [first name]".
2. A first line that proves you looked: a specific post, page or result of theirs.
3. One specific idea, tied to what you saw.
4. One low-effort question they can answer with "sure".

Example for a booking tool sold to a dental clinic:

> Subject: quick question, [Dr Lee]
> Tried to book a cleaning on your site at [8pm] on [Tuesday]. The form told me to call during office hours. I sketched [2] ways to take bookings after closing. Want me to send them?

Use a line like this only if you really did what it says.

- Don't sound needy or pushy. Back every claim with proof.
- Give value first: a short audit, sample copy, or a personal screen recording.
- Follow up with something useful for weeks. "We already have someone" is not a final no.
- Skip: income promises, posing as a buyer, and pushing on pain until it feels manipulative.

## Stefan Georgi: direct response and curiosity

### What direct response is

- Copy that asks for one clear action now: click, call, reply, book. It runs in ads, landing pages, sales letters, emails and DMs.
- A home page serves the brand. A landing page sells one thing, and ads point to it.

### The six-point checklist

1. It grabs attention at once (first line, subject, image).
2. It is about the reader and shows their gain fast.
3. It names a pain and promises a fix.
4. It often tells a story: feeling first, logic second.
5. It sounds like talking.
6. It ends with at least one clear call to action.

### Curiosity bullets (fascinations)

- A fascination is a short line that opens an itch and promises to scratch it if the reader keeps going. It hints at the answer without giving it.
- One big hook (the main promise) plus several small hooks (bullets) means fewer readers swim past. Georgi calls curiosity his biggest edge.
- **Alpha (opening):** after the main promise, add "Plus, in a moment I'll show you..." and 2 or 3 bullets.
- **Omega (close):** bullets describe what is inside the product, so acting is the only way to find out. Page numbers make it feel full: "Page [12]: ..."
- Add a hint: "Hint: it has nothing to do with [the obvious thing]."

The 11 bullet types, with formulas and generic examples:

| Type | Formula | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Why | Why [result] happens even when [you do the right thing] | Why easy runs can feel hard even when you sleep well |
| How | How to [get X] without [Y] | How to finish your first 5K without a single sprint session |
| When | When to [act], and when waiting costs you | When to file your quarterly taxes early, and when it costs you |
| What | What [insiders] know about [X] | What bookkeepers check first in a messy set of accounts |
| Secret | The real reason [X] | The real reason new runners quit in week three |
| List | The [3] signs that [X] | The 3 signs your receipt system will fail at tax time |
| Never | The one thing you should never [X] | The one thing never to say to a nervous dental patient |
| Contrarian | Think [X] works? Here's what does. | Think more miles make you faster? Here's what does instead. |
| Named oddity | A memorable name for a real method | The "[two-minute] rule" that stops you skipping runs |
| Speedy | [Result] in [short time] | Clean books in [one afternoon] |
| Plus | Main benefit, plus a second one | ...plus the [one] setting that keeps reminders out of spam |

Rules for bullets:
- Every bullet must point to something real in the product. Georgi invents demo bullets to teach; never ship one.
- Stacks suit long pages. In a short email or DM, use one curiosity line at most.
- Curiosity must still be clear. A riddle nobody can parse fails the two-second test.

### Context is king (his 2025 points)

- Copy is one step in a journey: stranger to first purchase, then buyer to repeat buyer through upsells and follow-up.
- The funnel is just AIDA: they see the ad, click, the page builds desire, they act.
- A good ad gets the right people to act, again and again. Clicks from the wrong people don't count.
- Write for how much the reader already knows about the problem and the fix.
- Match the platform. On YouTube, teaching beats a hard sell. An ad seen while reading news should lead to an article-style page.
- Keep the same feeling from ad to page to checkout. No funnel is perfect: guess, then test.
- Borrow the structure of copy that works for similar products, never its words.
- In his view AI drafts most copy now, and the human's edge is research, strategy and editing.

## Where they agree

- Show the reader what they get in the first line or two.
- Be concrete. Proof beats adjectives.
- Write the way people talk, in short lines.
- End with one clear next step. A reply counts.
- Borrow proven structures, then rewrite many times.
- Curiosity and contrast keep people reading.

## Where they disagree

- **Clarity vs curiosity.** Dry wants it understood in two seconds; Georgi builds itches that pay off later. Use clarity in cold, short messages and curiosity on long pages once someone is reading.
- **AI.** Dry says AI lacks taste and experience. Georgi says AI drafts and the human thinks. Tyson uses it to polish. Rule: draft freely, but every claim comes from the user's real facts.
- **Fear and hype.** Tyson and Georgi lean on pain and urgency. Dry uses facts, contrast and wit. For business buyers and wary readers, take Dry's lighter touch.
- **Price.** Tyson keeps price out of written outreach. Middle path: none in the first cold message, a plain answer when asked.

## Top takeaways

1. Run every line through the three tests.
2. First cold message: proof you looked, one idea, one tiny ask.
3. Lead with facts the user owns.
4. Match the next step to where the reader already is.
5. Never invent a number, a deadline, a story or a bullet.

## Check

1. Does each line pass the three tests, or is it filler?
2. Would a stranger get the main point in two seconds?
3. Does the first line make the reader want the second?
4. Is there exactly one clear next step?
5. Can every fact, number and curiosity bullet be backed up by the user?
