# Email

Based on Robert W. Bly's The Copywriter's Handbook (4th ed.), in our own words.

Use this file for marketing emails, cold emails, follow-up sequences and newsletters. For subject line ideas, also see `headlines.md`.

## How an email gets read

The reader gives an email a few seconds. Put the whole message and the link in the first lines, then add detail for people who want more. Getting into the inbox is half the work, so permission, a clean setup and few complaints matter as much as the words.

## Subject line and preview

1. **Treat the subject like the teaser on an envelope.** Short, curious, and not like an ad.
2. **Avoid ad words and spammy phrases,** such as "order now", "for free" or "advertisement" in odd places.
3. **Go easy on capitals.** They read as shouting.
4. **Write several and test them.** AI tools can produce many subject lines fast. Treat them as ideas to test, not answers.
5. **Make the opening words count.** The first line of the body is often what the reader sees next to the subject, so it should carry the message, not a greeting or filler.

Patterns: "[First name], your [specific thing]", "the same [20] questions", "Your [7pm] [calls]".

## Sender name

- Use a real person's name when the company is unknown.
- Use a brand or team name only once people know it.

## Body

1. **First paragraph: the whole message in small.** State the offer and put the link right there. Busy readers stop after a line or two.
2. **Then expand** with benefits, proof and detail for readers who want more.
3. **Repeat the offer and link at the end.**
4. **Use few links.** Most clicks go to the first two. Use three or fewer in warm email, and one or none in cold email.
5. **Keep lines short,** about 60 characters, so nothing wraps badly.
6. **Shorter usually wins.** If a buyer needs facts, keep the email short and put the detail on the landing page.
7. **Be helpful and specific, not hype.** Swap "best" and "quality" for a fact. Ask: who says so, besides us?
8. **Use "free" with care.** Bly's tests found it usually lifts response, even with spam filters. In cold email from a new domain, use it sparingly.
9. **Always include an easy opt-out,** and respect it.

## Call to action

- One ask per email.
- Make the easiest action a reply when you can: "Reply 'yes' and I'll send [the video]."
- Say exactly what happens after they act.

## Plain text or designed

- **Cold email:** plain text only.
- **Newsletters to people who signed up:** one column, dark text on a white background, one button.
- **Any email:** plain text or valid, simple HTML. No scripts.

## Getting delivered

1. **Permission first.** Mail people who expect you, or who have a clear business reason to hear from you.
2. **Ask readers to add you to their contacts,** in the welcome email.
3. **Set up SPF, DKIM and DMARC** for the sending domain.
4. **Accept bounces** and remove addresses that fail.
5. **Watch bounce, complaint and open rates** for each send. When one jumps, find out what changed. Too many complaints get you blocked.
6. **Test before big sends** for spammy wording.

## Cold email

Cold email goes to people who have not asked to hear from you. Its only goal is a reply.

1. **Keep it short.** Put the whole point in the first 2 lines.
2. **Aim for one thing: a reply.** Not a sale, not a click.
3. **Make line 1 about them.** Use something real and specific you saw. Never fake it.
4. **Send from a real person,** with honest sender details.
5. **One link at most.** Often none: ask a question they can answer in one word.
6. **No attachments** to people who don't know you.
7. **Plain text, no tracking.**
8. **End with an opt-out line.**
9. **Keep daily volume low from a new domain,** for example under 10 a day, until it is warmed up. To send more, use a separate sending domain and warm it up first.
10. **Check the rules** for cold email where the recipient is.

### Cold email skeleton

```
Subject: [specific thing about them]

Hi [Name],

[One real, specific thing you noticed about their business.]
[What that probably costs them.]

[Product] [does X], so [result they want].

Worth a [10]-minute look? Reply "yes" and I'll send a
[60]-second video.

[Your name], [Company]
Reply "stop" and I won't write again.
```

Example (made-up bookkeeping service writing to a cafe): line 1 might be "Saw you just opened a second site on [street]." Line 2: "Two tills usually means twice the receipts to sort each month."

### Bump

Send it after about 3 days, in the same thread:

```
Hi [Name], bumping this in case it got buried.
Short version: [one-line offer].
Worth a look, or should I close the loop?
```

## Follow-up sequence: give, give, offer, last call

### How it works

- Give free content first, capture the email, then send a short series that turns the reader into a buyer. 3 to 7 emails works best.
- Start with 1 or 2 "free touch" emails that only add value. Then ask for the order.
- Open each email by reminding them what they asked for. It builds goodwill and a sense of give-and-take.
- The best offer is a free trial that is truly free until it ends. Next best is a trial with a refund if they are not happy.
- The last email has a real deadline, and you keep to it.
- Bly's sample timing: day 1, 2, 4, 7, 14. The 4-email version below uses day 0, 2, 4 and 9.

### Email 1. Day 0. Give: deliver and add value

```
Subject: Your [free guide]

Hi [Name], here's the [guide] you asked for: [link].
The one to try today is [one quick tip, with its page].
It will [small, quick win].
Next time I'll send [the next useful thing].
[Your name]
```

### Email 2. Day 2. Give: teach

```
Subject: The [3] [mistakes or gaps] [people like them] miss most

Hi [Name], following up on the [guide] you downloaded.
[3] things slip through most often: 1) [ ], 2) [ ], 3) [ ].
The [guide] fixes 1 and 2. Number 3 is harder, because [reason].
That gap is why we built [Product]. More on that next time.
[Your name]
```

### Email 3. Day 4. Offer

```
Subject: [Fixing the hard one]

Hi [Name], last time I said [number 3] is the hardest to fix.
[Product] [does X] and [does Y], so [result].
It's [price], [cancel anytime or the real terms].
Here's [60] seconds of it working: [link].
Want me to set it up for a free [week]? Just reply "yes".
[Your name]
```

Offer the free trial only if you really do.

### Email 4. Day 9. Last call with a real deadline

```
Subject: Closing [the free trial] on [day]

Hi [Name], I'm holding [N] free setup slots this month and
closing them on [day, date]. If [the problem] costs you even
[one small loss] a month, [price] pays for itself.
Reply "yes" and I'll book a [10]-minute setup call.
Not the right time? Reply "later" and I'll check back in [month].
[Your name]
Reply "stop" and I won't email again.
```

Use only a real deadline and real slot numbers, and keep to them.

### Rules for the sequence

- One link per email. A reply is the easiest action.
- Stop the sequence the moment they reply, book or opt out.
- Track replies, bookings and opt-outs for each email. Change one thing at a time.

## Newsletters

- Practical, bite-size how-to tips beat opinion and long explainers. Put the best tip first. Link a tip to current news when you can.
- Reuse what you already have: news, case stories, posts.
- One newsletter serves two readers. Prospects need to be delighted, then moved to buy. Customers need tips for using the product and news of add-ons. Add a short trial offer to each issue.

## Check

1. Would the first 2 lines alone make them reply or click?
2. Is the subject short and curious, with no ad words?
3. Is there one clear ask, with at most one link (cold) or three (warm)?
4. Is every claim specific and true, with no "best" or "leading"?
5. Is there an easy opt-out, and is the send permission-based or kept to a low daily volume?
