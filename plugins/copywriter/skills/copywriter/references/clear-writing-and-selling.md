# Clear writing and selling

Based on Robert W. Bly's The Copywriter's Handbook (4th ed.), in our own words, plus ideas credited inline.

Use this file for the body of any selling piece: emails, landing pages, posts, scripts, letters and flyers.

## What copy is for

Copy has one job: to sell. Liked, funny or pretty does not count unless it moves people to act. Judge copy by results, not taste. A plain message that gets replies beats a clever one that gets praise.

Every piece must do four things, in this order:

1. Get noticed.
2. Be understood.
3. Persuade.
4. Ask for one clear next step.

Readers have less time and doubt more claims. Cut every spare word, speak to the one worry that matters, and prove instead of hype. Measure replies, clicks or sales so you know which version works.

## Clear-writing rules

1. **Write to one reader.** Picture one real person like the buyer. Use "you" far more than "we". Turn company talk into what the reader gets.
2. **Put the best point first.** The main promise goes in the headline and the first paragraph. Lesser points come later. Never bury the best offer.
3. **Start selling in line one.** Delete warm-ups like "In today's fast-moving world". The reader already knows their own market.
4. **Break it up.** Use numbers when order matters and bullets when it does not. Add subheads to long pieces. Keep paragraphs short.
5. **Follow the 4 S's.** Short words, short sentences, short paragraphs, short sections.
6. **Keep sentences short.** Aim for an average of about 14-16 words. Ads can go shorter. Split long sentences, and vary the length so it does not sound robotic.
7. **Choose small words.** "Use" not "utilise". "Get" not "obtain". "Buy" not "purchase". Big words annoy more than they impress.
8. **Test every bit of jargon.** Keep a technical term only if nearly every reader knows it and no plainer word is as exact. Otherwise explain it or drop it.
9. **Cut padding.** Remove repeats and doubled words. "Free gift" becomes "gift". "Can help you save" becomes "saves". The first draft is chatty. The edit makes it sharp.
10. **Be specific.** Collect twice as many facts as you will use, then pick the best. Vague copy means the writer had nothing to say.
11. **Sound like a person.** Use contractions, pronouns and everyday phrases. Ask: would I say this out loud to them?
12. **Use inclusive words.** Avoid gendered job titles. Use plurals or neutral terms.

Before and after (a bookkeeping service):

- Before: "Our firm delivers end-to-end financial administration solutions."
- After: "Send us your receipts. We do the books and tell you what you owe."

## Tricks that help rhythm

- It is fine to end a sentence with a preposition.
- Sentence fragments add pace. Like this.
- Start a sentence with "And" or "But" when it reads naturally.
- Use the odd one-line paragraph to wake the reader up.
- Highlight only a few key words (bold, underline, a P.S.). Highlight too much and nothing stands out.
- **Bullet rule:** be specific about the problem and mysterious about the answer. Give too much away and there is no reason to reply. Give too little and nobody is hooked.

## Screen notes

- Emails read best with 1-2 sentence paragraphs and lines of about 60 characters.
- Keep each web page to one topic.
- Web copy is easy to fix, so publish, measure and improve.

## Features into benefits

A feature is what the thing is. A benefit is what the buyer gains. Sell benefits.

1. List every feature in a left column.
2. For each one, ask "So what does the buyer gain?" Write the answer in a right column.
3. Make the strongest benefit the theme. Use the others in order of strength.

Example (a running app):

- Feature: "Adaptive training plans." Benefit: "Your plan gets easier on bad weeks, so you don't quit."
- Feature: "Audio coaching." Benefit: "You never have to look at your phone mid-run."

## The motivating sequence (5 steps)

Use this order for any piece that has room for a full argument.

1. **Get attention.** Lead with the single biggest benefit. Do not save it for the end.
2. **Show the need.** Make the problem felt, especially if the reader has not noticed it yet.
3. **Show you solve it.** Present the product as the answer to that exact need.
4. **Prove it.** Use benefits, customer words, fair comparisons with sources, test results and signs the business is solid and will last.
5. **Ask for action.** Name the next step and make it easy. Give a reason to act now. If they will not buy today, offer a smaller step such as a demo, a sample or a short call.

Related formulas say the same thing in other words: AIDA (attention, interest, desire, action), ACCA (awareness, comprehension, conviction, action) and the 4 P's (picture, promise, prove, push).

## Unique selling proposition (USP)

1. Make a clear promise: "Buy this and you get this."
2. It must be something rivals do not offer or do not say.
3. It must matter enough to win buyers. A tiny difference does not count.
4. If the product is not truly different, do one of these:
   - Claim a real benefit nobody else talks about.
   - Show the benefit in a vivid demo.
   - Own one use case where you are the best choice ("Call us when...").
5. **Positioning:** decide where you sit in the buyer's mind, often next to a name they know. Then still give the reasons to buy.

## Big promise plus backup promise

- A big promise grabs attention but can sound too good to be true.
- Add a smaller, easy-to-believe promise that is worth buying for on its own.
- Then even doubters think "I can't lose."

Example (a SaaS scheduling tool):

- Big: "Cut no-shows by [40%]."
- Backup: "And every client gets a reminder the day before, so nobody forgets."

Use honest comparisons only. Never frame true facts to suggest something false.

## Know the buyer: BDF (credit: Mark Ford)

Don Hauptman, quoted by Bly, put it simply: "Start with the prospect, not the product."

Before writing, write a short paragraph on each:

- **Beliefs:** what they think about the problem and about tools like yours.
- **Desires:** what they want to change.
- **Feelings:** how they feel day to day (stressed, proud, doubtful, fed up).

Reach them on three levels: logic (facts), emotion (fear, gain, pride) and personal (their exact situation). Personal hits hardest. Talk to real buyers when you can. Small details from customers often become the winning angle.

## Why people buy

Most buying motives fall into these groups:

- Gain: money, time, ease.
- Safety: security, fear of loss.
- Status: feeling important, different or right.
- Comfort and pleasure.
- Belonging: being liked and appreciated.
- Curiosity and learning.
- Guilt.

Pick the one or two that fit this buyer. Do not try to hit them all.

## Long copy or short copy

Length depends on the product, the reader and the job of the piece.

- **Go longer when** the price is high, the reader must buy straight from the page, the purchase is emotional and thought through, or the product is a want rather than a need.
- **Go shorter when** you only want a reply or a call (two-step selling), the reader is busy, or the product is already familiar.
- **Cold email is two-step.** Sell the reply, not the product. The landing page or the call does the rest.

## Research checklist

Do most of the work before writing. Specific facts sell.

1. **Gather what exists:** old ads, emails and pages, the website, customer reviews and messages, sales notes, competitor pages, and any data or test results. This often covers most of what you need.
2. **Ask about the product:**
   - What are all the features and benefits? Which matters most?
   - What is different from rivals? If nothing, what have rivals failed to mention?
   - What problem does it solve? How does it work? How reliable is it?
   - What does it cost, and how fast is setup?
   - Who uses it, and what do they say? What support and guarantee come with it?
3. **Ask about the buyer:**
   - Who buys, and what does it do for them? Why now?
   - What is their main worry: price, risk, speed or quality?
   - What drives them?
   - How many people must say yes?
   - Where will they see this message? Study that channel.
4. **Pick one goal for the piece:** get a reply, book a call, qualify a lead, get a sign-up, keep in touch or arm a salesperson. One main goal per piece.
5. **Check sources.** Trust named, current, cited sources with little bias. Note where each fact came from. If a fact is missing, leave a [bracket] placeholder. Never make one up.

## From notes to draft

1. Rewrite your notes in your own words. It helps you think and shrinks the pile.
2. For long pieces, outline first: a working headline plus the points you will cover. Get it approved before the full draft.
3. Write in three passes:
   - Pass 1: dump everything. Do not edit.
   - Pass 2: cut, reorder, read it aloud, strengthen the sell.
   - Pass 3: proofread and check every fact and name.
4. Write more than you need, then keep only the best.

## Getting unstuck

1. Define the problem in one sentence.
2. Gather more facts.
3. Keep feeding your general knowledge.
4. Look for new combinations of old ideas.
5. Sleep on it.
6. Run a checklist, such as a list of common objections.
7. Get feedback.
8. Team up with someone.
9. Do not judge ideas while you are still generating them.
10. If you freeze, write it as a message to a friend you are excited to tell.

## Final read-through

Does the body keep the headline's promise? Is it interesting, easy to read, believable, persuasive, specific, short, about the reader and smooth? Does it ask for action?

## Check

1. Is the main promise in the first line, with no warm-up?
2. Is every feature turned into a gain for the buyer?
3. Does it follow attention, need, solution, proof, action?
4. Is the one promise rivals do not make clear, with a believable backup?
5. Is the length right for the job, and is every fact sourced or in [brackets]?
