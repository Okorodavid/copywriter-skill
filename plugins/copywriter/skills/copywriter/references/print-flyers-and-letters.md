# Ads, letters, cold email, brochures and flyers

Based on Robert W. Bly's The Copywriter's Handbook (4th ed.), in our own words, plus ideas credited inline.

Use this file for ads, letters, cold emails, DMs, brochures, flyers and one-pagers.

## The nine tests of a good ad

Any ad, post or one-pager should pass all nine.

1. **Headline.** It offers a benefit, news, curiosity or a reward for reading.
2. **Visual (optional).** It shows the headline's benefit, with a caption.
3. **First paragraph.** It picks up the headline's thread straight away. Keep it under three lines.
4. **Layout.**
   - One main image, a big headline, readable body text under it, and subheads.
   - Dark text on a light background.
   - No slanted or reversed text, no tiny type, no clutter.
5. **Body order.** Most important point first, or a 1-2-3 list, or a story in time order, or problem then solution.
6. **Enough information.** Give the right people enough to take the next step. Ask: what do I want them to do, and what will make them do it?
7. **Interesting.** Write about the reader's life, problems and wants, with stories, news and free offers. Never about yourself or features with no gain.
8. **Believable.** Proof helps, but telling the truth matters most. Hype may win one sale but never a repeat customer.
9. **Asks for action.** Give one main way to respond and one or two backups (form, email, phone, text, QR code). A real address and phone number build trust.

### Other ad rules

- **Small ads:** call out the reader or their problem in the headline. For tiny ads, ask for an enquiry ("free details"), not an order, and track each with a code.
- **Repeat what works.** Big ads fade with each run. Small ads can run for years.
- **When stuck for an ad idea,** try a quiz, a letter-style ad, a case story, useful tips, the founder speaking, a challenge or a demo, as well as the headline types in headlines.md.

## Sales letters, cold email and DMs

Write as one person to another, using "I" and "you". The letter does the selling. Links and attachments carry the details. Push for a reply now. The opening line decides whether they read on.

Bly passes on an old direct-mail saying: "The letter sells; the brochure tells."

### Mechanics

1. Personalise when you can, but use their name once or twice, not over and over.
2. A greeting that names their group ("Dear clinic manager") beats a generic one.
3. A headline at the top is fine. So is starting with just the greeting.
4. Envelope teasers and subject lines:
   - Use a teaser only if it is hard to ignore. A weak one looks like junk.
   - Something free inside works best. Boasting works worst. Plain and personal often wins.
5. Cold email and DMs are two-step selling. Sell the reply, not the product.
6. Keep email paragraphs to 1-2 sentences.

Subject line test (a bookkeeping service):

- Boast (worst): "The best bookkeepers in the city"
- Benefit (better): "Get your weekends back from your books"
- Free inside (best): "A free tax-deduction check for [Business name]"

### 15 ways to open a sales letter or cold email

1. **Lead with the offer.** "Try the full app free for 30 days. No card needed."
2. **Lead with a free resource.** "Attached is a one-page checklist for closing your books in under an hour."
3. **Announce something new.** "As of this week, you can book a cleaning at [the clinic] by text."
4. **Tell a short story the reader sees themselves in.** "Last spring, a cafe owner told me she spent every Sunday night sorting receipts."
5. **Compliment their group.** "People who run their own studios work harder than almost anyone I know."
6. **Write as a peer.** "I ran a small design studio for six years, so I know how invoices pile up."
7. **A personal note from the founder.** "I built this tool after missing three payroll deadlines in one year."
8. **A surprising quote.** "'I thought bookkeeping software was only for accountants.' That is what [a client] told me before she tried ours."
9. **A question they want answered.** "How many new clients did you lose last month to slow replies?"
10. **Make it about them by name or detail.** "I saw [Business name] just opened a second location. Congratulations."
11. **Name their problem.** "Chasing late payments eats hours you could bill."
12. **State a strong benefit.** "You could finish month-end in one afternoon instead of three days."
13. **A human story with feeling.** "My first coach told me I would never run a mile. Last month I ran my tenth marathon."
14. **Share inside or early news.** "Before we announce it publicly, I wanted you to see our new pricing first."
15. **A contest or prize.** "Reply by Friday and you are entered to win a year of free service." (Use rarely, and follow local contest rules.)

Only use a story, number or quote if it is true. Put any figure you do not have yet in [brackets].

### Ways to lift replies

1. Always include one easy way to respond.
2. Offer something of value for replying: a free audit, sample, demo or trial.
3. Allow a "not now" reply, such as "If now is wrong, reply with a month and I will check back." It keeps the door open.
4. Add a real time limit or a real reason to act soon.
5. Add a P.S. that restates the offer. Many people read the P.S. first.
6. Give a guarantee.
7. For lead generation, say "no obligation" and promise no pushy follow-up.
8. If you only know their role, name the role at the top.
9. Repeat the offer at the point of reply.
10. Write terms in plain words.
11. Match the effort to their interest: do not oversell warm leads or undersell cold ones.

### Cold email outline (example: a SaaS scheduling tool)

- **Subject:** "No-shows at [Clinic name]"
- **Line 1, their problem:** "Most clinics lose [3-5] appointments a week to people who simply forget."
- **Line 2, the need:** "Each empty slot is paid staff time with no patient."
- **Line 3, the solution:** "Our tool texts each patient the day before and fills gaps from a waitlist."
- **Line 4, a low-risk ask:** "Can I send a 60-second video of how it works?"
- **P.S.:** "From [$X] a month. If it is not useful, just reply 'not now'."

## Brochures, flyers and PDFs

A brochure proves you are a real business and answers common questions in one place. It must still sell, not just describe. Write it for the exact step it plays in the sale, give it a selling cover, and end with a clear next step.

### Brochure and flyer rules

1. **Know where it fits in the sale:** leave-behind, counter piece, reply to an enquiry (load it with facts), insert with a letter, or a sales prop. If the next step is a meeting, send less, so there is still a reason to meet.
2. **Know what else exists.** If the website has the details, summarise and link. Put deep technical detail on a separate sheet.
3. **Know the reader.** Technical buyers want diagrams. Busy bosses want it short.
4. **Sell on the cover.** Use a benefit headline, and maybe the first lines of copy. Never just a logo. Never a gimmick unrelated to the product.
5. **Give complete information.** Prices, terms, guarantee and how to buy. Do not waste space on decoration.
6. **Order points the way the buyer wants to learn them,** for example what it is, benefits, how it works, how to buy. Question-and-answer format works well.
7. **Give each short section a subhead that tells part of the story.** No puns. Skimmers should get the pitch from subheads alone.
8. **Make visuals work.** Show the product in use, people or before-and-after. Caption every image: captions get read more than body text.
9. **End with the next step.** Make it easy, specific and ideally free, such as an audit or a demo.
10. **Do not forget the obvious.** Phone, email, web address, prices, hours, guarantee and correct spelling.
11. **Make it worth keeping.** Add a useful tip sheet, checklist or mini guide so they file it instead of throwing it away.

### Outlines

- **Product:** what it is and why care, benefits, stand-out features, how it works (with proof), who uses it, uses, options, price (on a separate insert if it changes often), specs, FAQs, about us, support, next step.
- **Service:** intro, what we do, benefits compared with the alternatives, our method, clients, testimonials, fees, terms and guarantee, team, next step.

### One-page flyer outline (example: a dental clinic)

- **Front:** benefit headline ("Same-week appointments, booked by text"), one photo with a caption, 3 subheads that tell the story.
- **Back:** how it works in 3 steps, prices, a short FAQ, the next step ("Text BOOK to [number]"), and a name and phone number.
- **Worth-keeping extra:** "5 signs you should see a dentist this month."

## Check

1. Does the headline carry a benefit, and does the first line continue it?
2. Does the opening hook with their problem, a benefit, news or an offer, and does it read like one person writing to another?
3. Is the layout clean, and can a skimmer get the pitch from subheads and captions?
4. Are price, terms, contact details and one clear next step all there and correct?
5. Is there something of value for replying, a "not now" option, and a P.S. or reason to act soon?
