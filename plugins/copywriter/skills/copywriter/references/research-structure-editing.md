# Research, structure and editing (the "writing to sell" process)

Notes in our own words from the talk "40 Years of Pro Copywriting Knowledge in 5 Hours" on the Copy That! YouTube channel. The speaker says it covers about three years of his own client work, so treat it as one writer's process. He credits the stages of awareness to Eugene Schwartz (*Breakthrough Advertising*), his research and editing methods to Joanna Wiebe (Copy Hackers), CUB editing to the book *Copy Logic*, and the five questions partly to Evaldo Albuquerque (*The 16-Word Sales Letter*).

Use it for sales pages, long emails, launches and any piece that needs research before writing.

## Core idea
Copy is research first and writing second. Find out what is already in the reader's head. Pick one idea that ties your product to their strongest wish. Then spend the body answering their doubts.

## 1. Stages of awareness
Work out where the reader is, open there, and move them one stage at a time. Never go back over a stage they have already passed.

| Stage | What they know | How to open |
|---|---|---|
| Unaware | Not even the problem | Call out who they are, with a little intrigue |
| Problem aware | The problem, not the fixes | Show you get the problem, and say a fix exists |
| Solution aware | The kind of fix, not your product | Name the fix they know, then show yours does it, or does it better |
| Product aware | Your product, not yet sold | Repeat your best promise and prove it |
| Most aware | Ready, just waiting | The offer itself, and the last doubt answered |

Research decides the stage. Existing customers are usually product aware or most aware. One headline can sometimes serve two stages ("how to avoid the biggest mistake new [X] make" works for unaware and problem-aware readers).

## 2. The Rule of One brief
Before writing any copy, write these four lines:
- **One reader:** age, situation, income, where they spend time online, their top reason to buy, and their awareness stage. Write for what they share with people like them, not one person's life story.
- **One idea:** one sentence with the big benefit tied to their strongest wish, plus why the product can deliver it. One idea fully believed beats ten half-believed. Every other benefit only backs it up.
- **One offer:** exactly what they get, how it arrives, the guarantee, any real bonuses and the true cost (money, time or an email address). They should be able to picture it.
- **One action:** write down the exact clicks. No extra links, pop-ups or newsletter boxes on that page.

## 3. Fact meets desire
List the product's facts. List the reader's pains, wishes and beliefs. Lead with the fact where the two lists overlap. A feature they don't care about is noise, however good it is. Turn real facts into benefits, but never invent a feature.

## 4. Customer-words research (voice of customer)
- **Where to look:** people polish their answers in surveys. Read where they talk freely instead: product reviews (yours and rivals'), Reddit, forums, YouTube comments, Quora and Facebook groups.
- **Sort the quotes,** word for word, into seven buckets:
  1. pains and fears;
  2. objections;
  3. wishes;
  4. firm beliefs (never argue with these);
  5. shakable beliefs (ones they'd like to be talked out of);
  6. other;
  7. repeated words and phrases.

  Note the source of each quote.
- **Summarise, rank, match.** Sum up each bucket in 3-10 short lines. Rank them all by how often and how strongly they come up. Match each line to a product benefit, and don't force a match. The top match becomes your one idea.
- **Write with them.** Put the matching quotes next to each point in your plan. Merge 2-3 of them into one short line that keeps their strongest word.
- **Look behind the stated wish.** People may ask for "cheaper", but the real pain is often the hassle.
- **Privacy:** use what people mean. Quote a real person word for word only with their permission.
- **Never invent customer quotes.** If you have no research, say so and write from what the user told you.

## 5. Competitor research
- Act as the buyer on fresh accounts, and note every ad and email you get.
- Keep a sheet for 2-3 rivals: their main message, what makes them different, the pain or wish they focus on, their channels and ads, and the praise and complaints in their reviews.
- An ad that has run for 3+ months is a clue it works, not proof.
- Also study brands that sell different things to the same buyers. They often use the right language.
- Borrow patterns and themes, never their words.

## 6. The five subconscious questions
By the end, the reader must be able to answer all five:
1. Why does this matter to me now?
2. Why isn't this something I already know?
3. What do I gain?
4. How does it deliver that?
5. Do I trust who's saying it?

Number 4 is the one most writers skip. Explain the mechanism in a line or two.

## 7. Plan before writing
- **Order the points** so each one leads to the next, moving one belief at a time (A to B to C). Drop themes that don't link to the one idea, even good ones.
- **Make a rough plan.** Under each point, note what you'll say and which proof you'll use, but not the final words. Put any good lines that come to you in quotes for later.

## 8. Show, don't tell (dimensionalize)
Take a flat benefit or pain and keep asking "what does this really mean to them?" until you reach a moment in their day. Then show that moment.
- **A benefit:** "needs less of your attention" becomes not checking urgent emails on holiday.
- **A pain:** "disappointed with your ads" becomes refreshing a stats page that won't move.

## 9. Lead, body, close
- **Headline:** it has three jobs. Grab the one reader, get the next line read, and set honest expectations for what follows. Build it from the one idea, then score it on the 4 U's.
- **Lead:** it ends when the reader thinks "OK, convince me". A strong lead can carry an average body, but nothing saves a weak lead.
- **Body:** it fights doubts. It isn't a pile of extra benefits, because each extra benefit thins out belief in the one idea. Every message either backs up the one idea or removes a real doubt.
- **Close:**
  1. Lay out the offer clearly, then ask for one action.
  2. Break the reader's problem into 2-3 parts, and show which part of the offer fixes each one.
  3. Make sure they get exactly what they expected the moment they act.
- **Summary P.S.:** use one on long pages, because people skip to the bottom. Recap the main benefit, the offer and the key answer to their doubt.

## 10. OCPB for each doubt
- **Objection:** say it out loud, or assume they're thinking it.
- **Claim:** a claim that answers it.
- **Proof:** evidence for the claim. The mechanism counts, not just testimonials.
- **Benefit:** what it means for them.

It can take two sentences or several paragraphs. For example, a lamp "can't run out of battery" (claim) because of its solar panel and hand crank (proof), so you never have the "forgot to charge it" moment again (benefit).

Only answer doubts the reader really has. Raising one they would never raise wastes words and hands them a reason not to buy.

## 11. Editing in two passes
1. **CUB (from *Copy Logic*).** At least three readers, ideally real buyers, mark lines only as Confusing, Unbelievable or Boring, with no advice. Fix the lines most of them marked first:
   - **Confusing:** cut it, or make it simpler if it's needed.
   - **Unbelievable:** cut it or prove it.
   - **Boring:** cut it, shorten it, or make it relevant.

   When no readers are available, mark the lines yourself, and suggest the user run the real test. Never claim readers reviewed it.
2. **"So what? Prove it."** Ask both questions of every line. If you can't say what a line does for the reader, cut it. If you can't prove a claim, cut it.

## 12. Style
- Write any way the reader understands that doesn't cost you their trust.
- Skip humour, puns and cultural references, because they don't travel and can cost belief.
- Use simple sentences with few commas, and use contractions.
- Keep paragraphs looking short.

## 13. Testing and strategy
- **Test big things,** not single words or fonts: the one idea, the headline and lead, the page structure, the offer layout.
- **Copy can't save a weak product.** Before writing, ask whether the product is different enough, whether the offer justifies the price, and whether the reader is ready to hear it.

## What not to copy from the talk
- **Headlines that invent a dispute or news story.** Keep the structure, drop the fake angle.
- **Endless "2 days left" discounts.** These are fake deadlines. Use real limits only.
- **Politics and "using prejudice" as levers.** Use real pains and wishes instead.
- **"Write as long as it takes" for every format.** It applies to sales pages; cold email and DM limits still win.
- **Free trials that charge automatically without saying so.** State the charge and how to cancel plainly.

## Check
- Is the opening right for the reader's awareness stage?
- Is there a Rule of One brief, with one reader, idea, offer and action?
- Are the reader's words from real research, or is it clearly marked that there was none?
- Can the reader answer all five questions, including how it works?
- Does each real doubt get OCPB, and does the copy raise no new doubts?
- Did every line pass "So what? Prove it" and a CUB read?
