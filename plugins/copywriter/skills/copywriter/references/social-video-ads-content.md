# Social posts, video, ads and content

Based on Robert W. Bly's The Copywriter's Handbook (4th ed.), in our own words.

Use this file for social posts, video and audio scripts, online ads, press releases, guides, blogs, case studies, reviewing copy and simple layout choices. For hooks, see `headlines.md`.

## Social media posts

1. **Know the aim:** teach, share a view, make an offer, start a talk, or show who you are. No aim, no post.
2. **Write to one person.** Warm and personal.
3. **Open with a strong first line** that stops your exact buyer.
4. **Keep it short.** Real value in 2 or 3 short sentences or paragraphs. Save long pieces for the blog.
5. **Give value even if nobody clicks.**
6. **End with an open question** (do they agree, what would they do) or one clear next step.
7. **Rewrite for each platform.** One topic, a different headline per platform, all linking to the same page. Never paste the same text everywhere.
8. **Use posts as the top of a funnel:** post, then a blog or landing page, then an email sign-up, then follow-up emails.
9. **Check every post on a phone,** before and after posting.

Example (running app), one idea on three platforms:
- LinkedIn: "Why [most] new runners quit in week 3, and the 5-minute fix."
- X: "Week 3 is where new runners quit. Not week 1."
- Facebook group: "Runners: what made you almost quit in your first month?"

**Platform notes**
- **LinkedIn post:** a focused headline, a few lines, a professional image, a link and call to action at the end.
- **LinkedIn profile:** a headline that says what results you get for clients, a banner that shows proof, services each with a specific result, a clear way to contact you.
- **X:** single posts under about 100 characters tend to work best. Use narrow hashtags, and check which spelling is most used.
- **Facebook:** use a business page unless you are the brand, with name, logo, contact details and what you offer.

**Habits:** reply to comments. Post on a schedule with themes people can expect. Mix tips and data with a little of the real story (the build, the setbacks). Skip politics, religion and other hot-button topics. In arguments, no insults, back views with evidence, and don't state opinions as facts.

## Short video and scripts

1. **One video, one topic:** one product, one idea, one offer.
2. **Get the brief first:** the one message, the problem solved, who appears, and where the viewer goes next.
3. **Hook in the first seconds.** Open on the viewer's problem, a striking image or something moving.
4. **Follow this flow:** introduce fast, name the problem, show the fix working, show life after, then one call to action.
5. **Show real outcomes on screen:** a report, a message, a calendar filling up.
6. **Talk to "you"** in plain, short words.
7. **Add captions to everything.** Many people watch with the sound off.
8. **Design for phones:** vertical or square, brand shown early.
9. **Plan in a table:** scene, what we see, what we hear. One sentence per scene first, full lines after.
10. **Read it aloud and time it.** Narration runs about 100 to 120 words a minute.

**Lengths:** social and DMs, 30 to 90 seconds. Home page, 2 to 3 minutes. B2B explainer, about 4. Product page, up to 5 to 7.

Skeleton (30 seconds, made-up dental clinic):

| Time | We see | We hear |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 5s | [The problem, shown] | "[Name the problem in one line.]" |
| 5 to 15s | [What it costs them] | "[Why it hurts.]" |
| 15 to 25s | [The product working] | "[Product] [does X]." |
| 25 to 30s | [Name and next step on screen] | "[One action.] [Short web address.]" |

**Long video sales letters:** break the viewer's pattern at the start, tell a story, use about 20% fewer facts than a written page, reveal the solution within a minute or two, use no more than two rounded numbers per sentence, and sound sure. Test long videos before judging them by taste.

## TV, radio and presentation video

1. **Sell, don't just entertain.** People liking an ad does not mean it sold them.
2. **Safest formats:** demonstration, testimonial, or a person talking to camera. Humour is risky: viewers remember the joke and forget the product.
3. **Pictures must sell with the sound off.** The words explain the pictures.
4. **The first 4 seconds decide it.** One idea per spot.
5. **Say the name and main benefit at least twice.** Back the key claim with on-screen text.
6. **Make the next step easy to remember:** a short web address or simple phone number.
7. **Script format:** what we see on the left, what we hear on the right.
8. **Audio:** paint pictures with sound. Repeat contact details. Ask sales staff what customers really worry about, then answer it.
9. **Presentation video:** 8 to 10 minutes is ideal, 20 at most. Write for the ear and repeat key points.
10. **Slides:** one picture per slide, or about 5 bullets of about 5 words. Plain backgrounds.

## Online ads

1. **Work out what a click is worth first.** If [20] clicks make one sale at [$1] each, a customer costs [$20]. Is the profit bigger? For list-building, base it on what one email address is worth over time.
2. **Send the click to a matching page,** never the home page.
3. **Start with a small test budget** to learn which image and hook work.
4. **Check current platform specs** before building. Specs change.

**Search ads:** pick specific keywords ("bookkeeping for cafes", not "accounting") and try "alternative to [competitor]". Put the keyword in the headline, then a question, a claim of superiority or an emotional hook. The description gives the benefit. Put the price in to filter out freebie seekers.

**Social ads:** the image does most of the work, so test many images with the same copy. The copy says what it is, why it helps and what to do next. Headlines: short, with a number, urgency, a benefit or a question. On LinkedIn, sponsored posts and messages suit B2B leads.

**Banners and newsletter ads:** banners build awareness more than clicks. Keep the text tiny: one benefit, a question or "see how". In newsletters, top slots and slots next to a related article do best. Write solo sends like the newsletter's own content, not hype.

## PR: press releases and articles

1. **A release is not an ad.** Editors use it only if it holds real news or useful advice for their readers. Buyers read it too.
2. **Tell the whole story in the headline.**
3. **Answer who, what, when, where, why and how in the first paragraph,** so it stands even if the rest is cut.
4. **Put bold claims inside a quote** from a named person.
5. **Cut hype words:** "best", "leading", "revolutionary".
6. **One page, two at most,** with a contact name, email and phone. Paste it in the email body, never as an attachment.
7. **Things that count as news:** a launch, a first customer, a result with numbers, a new market, a study you ran, an award, a partnership, a free tool or guide.
8. **Make news from advice or data:** "[5] ways [businesses] lose [customers] after hours", or findings from a test you ran.

**Articles:** case story, how-to, opinion or news scoop. How-to pieces sell without naming the product. Pitch with a short email: the angle, why readers care, why you can write it. The pitch is your sample, so polish it.

## Content marketing

1. **Aim high on the four levels:** why, what, how, and done for you. "How" and "done for you" (templates, scripts, checklists) are strongest.
2. **Set the goal first.** What belief must change before they buy?
3. **Ask three questions about the buyer:** what do they believe, want and feel? Build the headline from the answers.
4. **Tap a worry they already have.** Content can't create desire. It points an existing desire at your product.
5. **Go narrow.** One role, one step closer to buying. The right 50 readers beat 5,000 random ones.
6. **Rename the white paper** as a guide, special report or buyer's guide.
7. **Strong free offer? Lead with it.** Average one? Lead with the problem and product, and add the guide as a bonus.
8. **Avoid:** copy stitched from search results, stopping at "why", no expert input, no sources, opinion with no proof.

**Blogs:** post on a steady rhythm (weekly is good), take a stance, ask for replies, link to sources.

**Case studies:** who the customer is, the problem and its cost, options they tried, why they chose you, setup, how they use it, results with numbers, whether they'd recommend it. Get real quotes, push for numbers and get written sign-off.

## Working with AI or a writer, and reviewing copy

1. **Write it yourself** when you know the product deeply or it must sound like you. **Hand it off** when your time is worth more elsewhere.
2. **Use AI for volume on short pieces** like subject lines, and test the output.
3. **When hiring:** look for experience in your type of marketing, check samples, and agree fees, deadlines and revisions in writing.
4. **Review as the buyer, not as an editor.** The more people review copy, the weaker it tends to get. Keep approvers to 4 or fewer.
5. **Be specific:** "Put the [result] in the headline", not "needs more pizzazz". Write comments down.
6. **Sort edits:** about a quarter help, half make no difference, a quarter hurt. Accept the first two kinds and push back politely on the rest.
7. **Follow the rules for the field,** even when stricter wording lowers response.

## Design basics that affect copy

1. **Design exists to get the copy read and acted on.** Simple layouts usually sell best.
2. **Sketch a rough layout:** boxes for headline, image, bullets and button. Leave polish to a designer.
3. **One focal point.** The eye flows from headline and image, through the copy, to the action.
4. **Make the action the most obvious thing** after the headline.
5. **Readability first:** dark text on light, no text over busy photos, no tiny fonts, colour only to highlight a word.
6. **Real photos beat drawings.** No photo beats a bad one.
7. **Keep forms simple and honest.**
8. **Cut clutter.** If removing an element loses nothing, remove it.

## Check

1. Does the first line or first few seconds stop the exact buyer, even with the sound off?
2. Is there one topic and one clear next step?
3. Is it written for this platform, and checked on a phone?
4. Is every claim real and backed by a number, a demo or a named source?
5. Does the click lead to a matching page with nothing in the way?
