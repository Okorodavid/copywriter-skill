# Persuasion levers

Copy rules drawn from the seven levers in Robert Cialdini's "Influence" (new and expanded edition). Facts in [brackets] are placeholders. Fill them only with true numbers from the user, or cut the line.

## The shortcut idea (click, run)

- Busy people decide most things on autopilot. One cue sets off the whole habit: a price, a title, a crowd, a "last one".
- The cue clicks, the habit runs. Whoever controls the cue steers the answer.
- These shortcuts are usually right. That is why they work, and why faking one is fraud.
- Example from the book: a shop's staff doubled the price of slow-selling turquoise jewellery by mistake, and it sold out. Buyers read price as quality.
- Tired or rushed readers lean hardest on a single cue. Put the strongest true cue in the first line.

### Use the levers in order

| Job | When | Levers |
|---|---|---|
| Build the relationship | First contact | Reciprocation, liking, unity |
| Remove doubt | Interested but unsure | Social proof, authority |
| Get action | Agreed but not moved | Commitment, scarcity |

- In a cold message, do job 1. Save proof for the next message, and deadlines for last.

### Contrast and anchors

- Show the fuller, real option first. The second looks lighter beside it.
- Show what the problem costs before the price.
- Offer add-ons after the main yes.
- Don't price so low it reads as "probably bad".
- Never add an option nobody should buy just to flatter another.

## 1. Reciprocation

**What it is:** people feel they should repay what they receive. Give first, ask second.

**Why it works:** every culture teaches repaying favours, and owing feels bad. In the book, a small gift sent with a survey beat a much bigger reward promised for finishing it.

**How to use it:**
- Give something useful before any ask: a short audit, a sample, a custom report, a recorded walkthrough.
- Tailor it. "[3] questions your site doesn't answer" beats a generic guide.
- Say plainly it is free, with no catch.
- Ask soon after, and keep the ask small: a reply, a short look, a trial.
- Fix complaints fast and in person. A quick fix feels like a gift.
- Example, bookkeeping service: "You have [2] filing deadlines in [March]. Here they are, no strings." Later: "Want reminders for the rest of the year?"

### Rejection then retreat

- Ask for the larger real option first. If they say no, step down to a smaller real one. People feel they should answer a concession with one of their own.
- In the book, asking for a long volunteering commitment (refused) and then a single zoo trip tripled the yes rate.
- Example: "The full plan covers all [3] locations. If that's too much for now, we can start with one on the same terms."
- An absurd first ask looks like bad faith and kills the effect.

**The honest line:** the gift must be worth having even if they never buy. The bigger option must be one you would gladly deliver. Never hint "but I gave you this".

## 2. Liking

**What it is:** we say yes more easily to people we like.

**Why it works:** liking lowers our guard, so we check the offer less. It grows from similarity, honest praise, familiarity, working together and links to good news. In the book, guests at home product parties bought mostly because of their bond with the host.

**How to use it:**
- Open with real, specific common ground: same town, same trade, the same problem.
- Use the reader's own words for their work.
- Give one specific, true compliment. "Your reviews keep saying you call back the same day" beats "love what you do".
- Praise the trait you want more of: "You clearly care about getting back to people fast."
- Use a warm intro if one exists: "[Name] suggested I get in touch."
- Stand on their side, against the problem.
- Lead with gains. If you raise a problem, give the fix in the same breath.
- Look tidy: clear photo, neat page, real logo.

**The honest line:** claim only true common ground, praise only what is real, name a referrer only with their OK. Liking earns the reply; the offer must still win.

## 3. Social proof

**What it is:** people work out what is right by watching others, most of all others like them.

**Why it works:** following the crowd is usually a safe guess. It pulls hardest when the reader is unsure, when many people do it, and when they are similar. In the book, restaurants sold more of the dishes labelled "most popular".

**How to use it:**
- Label the true favourite: "Most clients start here", once true.
- Use real, specific counts: "[6] of our [7] clinics renewed".
- Match the proof to the reader. A dental clinic wants proof from clinics.
- Several small examples beat one big claim.
- Slip proof in sideways: "We're hiring to keep up" reads as news.
- Put proof near the price and the call to action.
- Never make the bad habit sound normal. In the book, a sign saying many visitors steal petrified wood made theft worse. Write "Busy clinics confirm every booking by text", not "Most clinics lose bookings".
- If the good habit is rare but growing, say it is growing.
- End with one clear step for one named person.

**The honest line:** no fake reviews, made-up user counts, staff posing as customers, or "[12] people viewing now" counters. Testimonials only from real clients, with permission.

## 4. Authority

**What it is:** people follow credible experts: people who know the subject and can be trusted.

**Why it works:** listening to experts usually pays, so we react to the signs of expertise without checking. Expert advice is welcomed; orders get pushback.

**How to use it:**
- Show expertise, don't claim it: what you built, ran or measured, in numbers.
- Do a short teardown of the reader's own situation. The work is the credential.
- Quote real outside sources: regulators, research, official docs. Several beat one.
- Admit a real weakness first, then your strongest point: "We're new, so no case studies yet. That's why the first month is free if it doesn't work."
- Argue a little against your own interest: "With under [20] clients, the basic plan is enough."
- Recommend, don't order: "If I ran your front desk, I'd switch on reminders first."
- Show mixed reviews too. In the book, ratings a little under perfect sold better than near-perfect ones.

**The honest line:** no fake titles, "as seen in" logos, stock-photo experts or implied endorsements. A weakness must be real, not a hidden boast.

## 5. Scarcity

**What it is:** things seem worth more when rare or slipping away.

**Why it works:** rare things are often better, a loss stings more than an equal gain pleases, and losing a freedom makes us want it more. In the book, people rated the same cookies higher from a nearly empty jar, and higher still when others had just taken the rest.

**How to use it:**
- Make the limit real, then state it with its reason: "We take [4] new clients a month, because each setup is done by hand."
- Prefer limited supply over limited time.
- Frame in losses when true: "Each unanswered booking request is a patient who tries the next clinic."
- If supply is unlimited, sell what is unique and what they miss without it.
- Present the deal as theirs, then the one step to keep it: "Your account is set up. Reply yes and it goes live Monday."
- Give early clients a lasting perk, like a locked price, and never take it back.

**The honest line:** no fake "only 2 left", timers that reset, invented rival buyers, or "final" deadlines that move. Would you be fine if the reader saw exactly how the limit works?

## 6. Commitment and consistency

**What it is:** once people take a stand, they want to act in line with it.

**Why it works:** consistency looks strong and saves thinking, and people judge who they are by what they have done. Commitments stick best when active, public, a little effortful and freely chosen. In the book's foot-in-the-door study, people who agreed to a tiny request were far more willing to accept a big related one later.

**How to use it:**
- Never ask for the sale first. Climb a ladder of small, related yeses.
- Ask "will you" and wait. A restaurant cut no-shows by asking "Will you call if your plans change?" and pausing for a yes.
- Get their own reason or number: "Roughly how many a week?"
- Make it active: they type, tick or reply. No pre-ticked boxes.
- Cut step one of any form to 2 or 3 fields.
- Give a label to live up to: "You're one of the few clinics that calls back the same day."
- Keep incentives small, so they feel they chose.

### The small-yes ladder

Example: a booking tool for a dental clinic.

| Step | Ask | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | "Do you get booking requests after closing?" | Easy yes |
| 2 | "Roughly how many a week?" | Their number |
| 3 | "If [2] of those booked, worth fixing?" | Predict, then ask |
| 4 | "Want to try booking on your site tonight?" | Active |
| 5 | "Send your hours and top questions. I'll set up a free [7]-day test." | Small effort |
| 6 | "Will you look at the results with me Friday?" Then wait. | Spoken promise |
| 7 | "You said [2] lost bookings a week. Keep it on at [the same price]?" | Consistent close |

**The honest line:** never change a term after a yes. No hidden auto-renew, fake surveys, or claiming they agreed when they didn't.

## 7. Unity

**What it is:** people say yes to someone they see as "one of us": shared place, trade, family or team, not just shared tastes.

**Why it works:** insiders get more trust and help, and it is strongest when the shared identity is top of mind. It grows from belonging together and acting together. In the book, people asked for "advice" on a new restaurant wanted to eat there more than people asked for an "opinion".

**How to use it:**
- Name a shared identity you truly have, early: "fellow small business", "another solo founder".
- Use place: the town or region.
- Ask for advice, not opinions.
- Build it together: let them shape the FAQ, the rules or the tone.
- Act together: a live setup call where you both click through it.
- Name a real common enemy: paperwork, no-shows, spam.

**The honest line:** claim only a group you belong to. Faked belonging is how affinity fraud works. Would the offer stand if the "we" line were cut?

## Using social proof, authority and scarcity with zero customers

- **Social proof:** show proof of work (real output from your own test runs, a live demo, a sample result), cite an honest trend with a source, or say "You'd be one of the first [5]". Never imply customers you don't have.
- **Authority:** say "we're new" first, then the strongest point straight after, such as a guarantee or a pay-only-if-it-works term. Show the work: a teardown of their situation, or your method in three lines.
- **Scarcity:** run a small founding group with a real reason for the cap, offer one client per area only if you will hold to it, and keep any founder perk.

## Add a reason, offer a way out

- **Add a reason.** Put "because" and a true reason in every ask. In a study by Langer, asking to skip a photocopier queue worked far better with a reason, even a thin one.
  - "Could I send a 2-minute video, because I spotted [a broken booking link] on your contact page?"
- **Offer a way out.** Tell them they are free to say no. Across 42 experiments in the book, a line like this raised compliance, often doubling it.
  - "No pressure if the timing is wrong." "Fine to say no, and I won't chase."
- Both must be true: a real reason, and you really won't chase.

## Check

1. Does each lever rest on a true fact the reader could check?
2. Does the lever fit the job: relationship first, doubt second, action last?
3. Can a skimmer get one strong cue from the first line?
4. Does every ask carry a real reason and a real way out?
5. Would you be comfortable if the reader learned exactly how each claim, limit and number works?
