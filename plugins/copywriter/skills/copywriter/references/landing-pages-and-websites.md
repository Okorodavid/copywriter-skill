# Landing pages and websites

Based on Robert W. Bly's The Copywriter's Handbook (4th ed.), in our own words.

Use this file when you write a website, a service page or a landing page. For headlines, see `headlines.md`. For the emails that follow a sign-up, see `email.md`.

## Websites

### Start with the site's job

1. **Name one business goal before you write.** Examples: answer a buyer's questions fast and book a call, sell a product, or collect email addresses. Traffic on its own is not a goal.
2. **Collect emails from people not ready to buy.** Offer a free guide, checklist, report or newsletter in return for an email address. Then follow up by email. Most online sales come from emailing people who already know you.
3. **Test traffic sources small and cheap.** Try many in small doses. Drop what fails and grow what works.

### Pages to include

- **Home**, with one clear call to action.
- **About**: who is behind it and why they are worth trusting.
- **One page per product or service.**
- **Articles or resources.**
- **Contact.**

For a full company site, use separate pages, not one long single page. Separate pages are easier to find in search and easier to move around. A sales landing page is the exception (see below).

### Two kinds of content

Cover both on every product or service page:

- **What buyers need to know:** what it does, how it works, the price, who is behind it.
- **What convinces them:** testimonials, case stories, results with numbers, credentials.

### Home page

- Say what you do in one line, then show one button.
- No rotating banners (sliders). Few people click them, they slow the page and they work badly on phones.

### Order for a selling page

Write in this order: get attention, state the problem, show the solution, prove it, ask for action. Proof can fill several sections in a row, such as testimonials, then how it works, then results.

### Know which format you are writing

Each has a different length and job: long sales page, short sign-up page, order or booking page, short teaser email, lead email, follow-up email series, small text ad, banner, exit pop-up.

### Writing for search

1. **Give each page 2 or 3 key phrases** of 2 to 4 words. Pick phrases a buyer would really type, such as "bookkeeping for cafes" rather than "financial services".
2. **Put the main phrase near the start of the headline.**
3. **Title tag:** up to about 60 characters, main phrase first.
4. **Meta description:** up to about 155 characters, main phrase first.
5. **Never stuff keywords.** It reads badly and search engines punish it.

Example title tag for a made-up firm: "Bookkeeping for Cafes in [City] | [Firm name]".

## Landing pages

### The one job

A landing page turns a click into one action: book a call, start a trial, ask for a quote, download a guide. Measure it by conversion rate (people who act divided by visitors). Every part of the page either helps that one action or gets cut.

### Rules

1. **Put trust on the first screen.** Before any scrolling, show a client logo, a short strong testimonial or a credential. Online visitors start out doubtful.
2. **Make one big promise or raise real curiosity in the headline,** so the right reader keeps going.
3. **Open with emotion.** Name how the reader feels about the problem right now. Then show the product, or the free offer, as the fix.
4. **Lead with a strong free offer.** If you have one, put it in the headline: "Free [7]-day trial" or "Free [audit]".
5. **Remove the menu and every other link.** The only choices should be to act or to leave.
6. **Use many bullets.** Each one names a feature, then what it means for the reader. Cover everything a buyer would want to know.
   - Example (running app): "Plans each week around your last run, so you never train hard on tired legs."
7. **Use lots of testimonials in the customer's own words.** Their words are more specific and more believable than anything you would write for them. Video testimonials should play only when clicked.
8. **Write like one person talking to another,** even for technical buyers. No brochure voice.
9. **Name the person behind the offer** and say why they are credible.
10. **Prove every claim** with a number, a screenshot, a demo or a real quote. If there is no proof yet, leave that section out. Never invent proof.
11. **Keep it current.** Old dates and stale facts kill trust. Tie the copy to what is happening now when you can.
12. **Remove the risk.** State the guarantee, "no obligation" or "cancel anytime" plainly, right next to the button.
13. **Make the button big, bold and high contrast.** You can also link key phrases in the text to the same action. Offer a phone or chat option for people who prefer to talk.
14. **Catch people who leave.** Offer something small, such as a checklist, for their email in a slide-in box, not a pop-up that blocks the page.
15. **Keep it readable.** Dark text on a light background. A clean layout. Nothing that makes reading hard.
16. **Build one page per audience or offer.** More focused pages bring more leads than one page for everyone. A dental clinic might run one page for emergency visits and one for teeth whitening.
17. **Test traffic in small amounts.** Try search ads, partners and affiliates, newsletter ads, email, and referrals from happy users. Track the cost per lead for each.

### Headline patterns

- **Promise:** "[Result the reader wants] in [time], without [the pain they hate]."
- **Question:** "How many [lost customers] did your [business] miss last [week]?"
- **Offer first:** "Free [trial or sample]: [what they get], before you pay anything."

Example for a made-up dental clinic: "Toothache? See a dentist at [Clinic name] today."

## Default 12-section landing page

Use this order unless the offer needs something else. Drop any section you cannot fill honestly. Keep every [bracket] as a placeholder until you have the real fact.

1. **Top bar.** Logo and one button: "[Book a call]". No menu.
2. **Hero.**
   - Headline: the main promise, such as "[Outcome the reader wants], [how fast or how easy]."
   - One line under it on what happens next: "[What the product does once they act]."
   - The main button, plus one real image of the product working (a screenshot or a photo).
   - One trust signal under the button: a client logo, a short quote or a credential. If you have none yet, use a true line about who it is built for.
3. **The pain.** 2 or 3 short lines on how the problem feels today. Use real data if you have it.
4. **How it works, in 3 steps.** "[They do X]. [The product does Y]. [They get Z]."
5. **Demo video.** Plays only when clicked, with captions.
6. **Feature-benefit bullets, 5 to 7.** "[Feature], so [what it means for them]."
7. **Proof.** Real numbers, such as [N] customers, [result] or [time saved], plus a named quote. Leave this section out rather than fake it.
8. **Objections as an FAQ.** Answer the 3 to 5 worries buyers really raise:
   - "Will it [the thing they fear]?"
   - "Does it work with [a tool they already use]?"
   - "What about [privacy or contracts]?"
   - "What if I want [a human, or to cancel]?"
9. **Pricing.** One to three clear options. Put "[Cancel anytime]" or the real trial terms next to each button.
10. **Risk reducer.** The guarantee or free trial in one plain sentence. State only terms you really offer.
11. **Final call to action.** Repeat the headline promise and the button.
12. **Exit catcher.** For people leaving: "Not ready? Get [the free checklist or guide]." Email field only.

## Check

1. Can you say the page's single goal in one line, and is there only one action?
2. Is there a trust signal on the first screen?
3. Does the headline make one big promise or raise real curiosity for the right reader?
4. Is every claim backed by a number, a screenshot, a demo or a real quote?
5. Is the risk reducer right next to the button?
